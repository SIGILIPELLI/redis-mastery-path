    04 · Geospatial Indexes¶

    "Find the ten nearest drivers", "which stores are within 5 km?" and "how far is the user from the restaurant?" are questions about points on a map, and Redis answers them with a handful of GEO* commands. There's no separate geo type. A geospatial index is a sorted set whose scores encode each point's location, which lets everything from Level 1 · 08 work on it too.

    Adding and reading points¶

    Coordinates go longitude first, then latitude. That's the opposite of how most people say them and the opposite of Google Maps' "lat, lng" display, so it's the most common source of geo bugs:

    127.0.0.1:6379> GEOADD cafes -9.1393 38.7223 lisbon-baixa -9.1427 38.7139 lisbon-cais -8.6291 41.1579 porto-ribeira -9.1500 38.7369 lisbon-saldanha
(integer) 4
127.0.0.1:6379> GEOPOS cafes lisbon-baixa
1) 1) "-9.139302670955658"
   2) "38.72230020357074"

    GEOPOS doesn't return exactly what went in. The location is quantized (more on that below), and the difference is tiny. For a random point in the benchmark later in this lesson, the stored and returned positions differed by 2.6 cm.

    Valid latitudes stop short of the poles:

    127.0.0.1:6379> GEOADD cafes 0 89 north-pole
(error) ERR invalid longitude,latitude pair 0.000000,89.000000

    The limit is ±85.05112878°, the same cutoff used by Web Mercator maps.

    GEOADD takes the same NX/XX/CH flags as ZADD. XX updates the position of an existing member, which is what a moving driver's location ping does:

    127.0.0.1:6379> GEOADD cafes XX -9.1395 38.7224 lisbon-baixa
(integer) 0

    Distances¶

    127.0.0.1:6379> GEODIST cafes lisbon-baixa porto-ribeira km
"274.3731"
127.0.0.1:6379> GEODIST cafes lisbon-baixa lisbon-cais m
"979.6791"

    Units are m, km, mi and ft.

    Searching¶

    GEOSEARCH finds members inside a circle (BYRADIUS) or a rectangle (BYBOX), centred either on a coordinate (FROMLONLAT) or on an existing member (FROMMEMBER):

    127.0.0.1:6379> GEOSEARCH cafes FROMLONLAT -9.1400 38.7200 BYRADIUS 2 km ASC WITHDIST
1) 1) "lisbon-baixa"
   2) "0.2629"
2) 1) "lisbon-cais"
   2) "0.7177"
127.0.0.1:6379> GEOSEARCH cafes FROMMEMBER lisbon-baixa BYRADIUS 5 km ASC COUNT 2 WITHDIST WITHCOORD
1) 1) "lisbon-baixa"
   2) "0.0000"
   3) 1) "-9.139302670955658"
      2) "38.72230020357074"
2) 1) "lisbon-cais"
   2) "0.9797"
   3) 1) "-9.142698347568512"
      2) "38.71390013764869"
127.0.0.1:6379> GEOSEARCH cafes FROMLONLAT -9.14 38.72 BYBOX 4 4 km ASC
1) "lisbon-baixa"
2) "lisbon-cais"
3) "lisbon-saldanha"
    • ASC sorts nearest first. Without it, the order isn't defined.
    • COUNT n limits the results, which is how you get "nearest N".
    • WITHDIST and WITHCOORD add the distance (in the query's unit) and the position.
    • FROMMEMBER includes the member itself at distance 0. Skip it in your code if you want its "neighbours".
    • BYBOX 4 4 km is a 4 km × 4 km box centred on the point. Saldanha, about 2 km north, falls inside the box but outside a 2 km circle.

    GEOSEARCHSTORE saves the results into another key. With STOREDIST, the scores of the new sorted set are the distances, so the result is a ready-sorted "by distance" list:

    127.0.0.1:6379> GEOSEARCHSTORE near:me cafes FROMLONLAT -9.14 38.72 BYRADIUS 3 km STOREDIST
(integer) 3
127.0.0.1:6379> ZRANGE near:me 0 -1 WITHSCORES
1) "lisbon-baixa"
2) "0.2629022054797619"
3) "lisbon-cais"
4) "0.7177408007183564"
5) "lisbon-saldanha"
6) "2.070324830252379"

    (GEORADIUS and GEORADIUSBYMEMBER are the older commands that GEOSEARCH replaces. You'll still see them in existing code.)

    It's a sorted set¶

    127.0.0.1:6379> TYPE cafes
zset
127.0.0.1:6379> ZSCORE cafes lisbon-baixa
"1966377783327353"
127.0.0.1:6379> ZRANGE cafes 0 -1
1) "lisbon-cais"
2) "lisbon-saldanha"
3) "lisbon-baixa"
4) "porto-ribeira"
127.0.0.1:6379> ZREM cafes lisbon-cais
(integer) 1

    There's no GEODEL. You remove a point with ZREM, count with ZCARD, and expire the whole index with EXPIRE. The score is a 52-bit geohash of the location stored as an integer. Points that are near each other usually have nearby scores, which is what makes searching fast. GEOHASH returns the standard base32 text form, usable with other geohash tools:

    127.0.0.1:6379> GEOHASH cafes lisbon-baixa porto-ribeira
1) "eycs210vwz0"
2) "ez3f5ss6pk0"

    A million drivers¶

    Load 1,000,000 random points across mainland Portugal and time a few searches around central Lisbon:

    import redis, random, time
r = redis.Redis(decode_responses=True)
random.seed(11)
pts = [(random.uniform(-9.5, -6.2), random.uniform(37.0, 42.1)) for _ in range(1_000_000)]
p = r.pipeline(transaction=False)
for i in range(0, len(pts), 5000):
    args = []
    for j, (lon, lat) in enumerate(pts[i:i+5000], start=i):
        args += [lon, lat, f"driver:{j}"]
    p.geoadd("drivers", args); p.execute()
print("members", r.zcard("drivers"), "encoding", r.object("encoding", "drivers"),
      "memory", r.memory_usage("drivers"))

def t(label, fn, n=200):
    s = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(n): res = fn()
    print(f"{label:52} {(time.perf_counter()-s)/n*1e6:8.1f} us -> {len(res)} results")

q = dict(longitude=-9.14, latitude=38.72, unit="km")
t("GEOSEARCH 1 km, ASC COUNT 10", lambda: r.geosearch("drivers", radius=1, sort="ASC", count=10, **q))
t("GEOSEARCH 10 km, ASC COUNT 10", lambda: r.geosearch("drivers", radius=10, sort="ASC", count=10, **q))
t("GEOSEARCH 10 km, ASC COUNT 10 ANY", lambda: r.geosearch("drivers", radius=10, sort="ASC", count=10, any=True, **q))
t("GEOSEARCH 10 km, all results", lambda: r.geosearch("drivers", radius=10, **q), n=20)

    members 1000000 encoding skiplist memory 77722402
GEOSEARCH 1 km, ASC COUNT 10                            217.5 us -> 10 results
GEOSEARCH 10 km, ASC COUNT 10                           840.8 us -> 10 results
GEOSEARCH 10 km, ASC COUNT 10 ANY                       190.6 us -> 10 results
GEOSEARCH 10 km, all results                            939.0 us -> 1799 results

    The interesting line is the second one. "Nearest 10 within 10 km" took 841 µs even though it returned only 10 results. To guarantee the ten nearest, Redis has to collect and sort every candidate in the radius (1,799 of them) before it can keep ten. Adding ANY lets Redis stop as soon as it has found ten matches, which took the time back down to 191 µs. The catch is that those ten are some matches within 10 km, sorted among themselves, and not necessarily the ten nearest.

    The practical pattern for "nearest N" is therefore to start with a small radius and widen it only if you don't get enough results (1 km, then 3 km, then 10 km). In dense areas the small query succeeds immediately, and in sparse areas the wide one has few candidates anyway.

    How accurate are the distances?¶

    Redis models the Earth as a sphere. Compared with geodesic distances on the WGS84 ellipsoid (computed with the geographiclib package) for the same stored positions:

    lisbon->porto: Redis   274.373 km  WGS84 ellipsoid   273.925 km  diff +0.164%
lisbon->tokyo: Redis 11145.939 km  WGS84 ellipsoid 11166.662 km  diff -0.186%

    An error of about 0.2% (450 m over 274 km, 21 km over 11,000 km) is irrelevant for "drivers near me" and wrong for surveying, aviation or anything legally binding. The search radius itself carries the same approximation. A point right at the edge of a radius may land on either side of it.

    How It Actually Works¶

    GEOADD interleaves the bits of the quantized longitude and latitude into one 52-bit integer, a geohash, and stores it as the member's sorted-set score. Twenty-six bits per axis divide the world into cells of roughly half a metre, which is where the centimetre-level GEOPOS difference comes from. A 52-bit integer also fits exactly in a double's 53-bit mantissa, so the score loses nothing.

    Interleaving bits creates a Z-order curve. Points that share a long geohash prefix are close together, and a prefix corresponds to a rectangular cell. To search a radius, Redis picks a geohash precision whose cells are about the size of the radius, computes the cell containing the centre plus its 8 neighbours, and turns each cell into a score range. Each range is an O(log N) seek in the skip list followed by a scan of the members in that cell. Each candidate's exact distance is then computed with the haversine formula on a sphere of radius 6,372,797.6 m, and candidates outside the circle or box are dropped. That's why the cost depends on how many points fall in the 9 cells, not on the total size of the index, and why a 10 km search in a dense city has to examine thousands of candidates.

    Since the index is just a sorted set, it inherits everything else: replication, persistence, MULTI, Lua, and the memory profile of a large sorted set (about 78 bytes per point here).

    Common mistakes¶

    • Latitude first. It's GEOADD key longitude latitude member, every time.
    • Assuming "nearest N" is cheap with a big radius. Without ANY, Redis sorts every candidate in the radius. Widen progressively.
    • Treating distances as survey-grade. The spherical model was 0.16–0.19% off here.
    • Forgetting stale positions. A driver who went offline stays in the index. Store a last-seen time and remove members, or keep one index per time bucket and expire old buckets.
    • One global index in Cluster for a hot use case. A sorted set lives on a single node. Shard by region (one key per city) if one node can't keep up.
    • Expecting polygons. Redis core supports circles and boxes. For polygons, or for combining geo with other filters, the query engine (Level 3 · 09) has geo fields.

    Exercise¶

    1. Build a "drivers near me" service. Drivers report positions every few seconds (GEOADD XX, with NX for new drivers), and riders ask for the nearest 5 within an expanding radius (1, 3, 10 km). Track last-seen in a separate sorted set and remove drivers silent for more than 60 s.
    2. Measure how the 10 km ASC COUNT 10 query time changes as you go from 100,000 to 3,000,000 points in the same area, with and without ANY.
    3. Verify the ±85.05112878° latitude limit experimentally. What's the largest latitude GEOADD accepts, and what does GEOPOS return for it?
    4. Find two points whose GEOSEARCH BYRADIUS result disagrees with an exact ellipsoidal calculation (one inside by one measure and outside by the other). How far from the radius boundary must a point be for this to happen?
